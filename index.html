The problem

Everything a model knows is frozen into its weights

Stale

Knowledge stops at the training cutoff. Policies, prices and people keep changing.

Private

It never saw your wiki, your tickets, your contracts, and it shouldn’t.

Unsourced

It can’t say where an answer came from, and it fills gaps fluently.

Fine-tuning is good at teaching style and format. It is a slow, costly and still uncited way to add or update facts.

Three failure modes of parametric memory. The Air Canada bot hit all three in one go: the answer was specific to the company (private), it’s the kind of thing that changes (stale), and the bot gave no source a customer could check (unsourced). Pre-empt the obvious question: “why not just fine-tune on the docs?” Fine-tuning shapes behaviour well, but injecting new facts is unreliable, has to be redone every time the docs change, and still gives no citations.

The core idea

Turn a closed-book exam into an open-book one

Closed book

answer = LLM(question)

Open book · RAG

answer = LLM(question, retrieve(question))

Knowledge now lives in an index you can read, update, restrict and cite, not only in the weights.

Lewis et al., NeurIPS 2020

p(y | x) ≈ Σz ∈ top-k(x)
pη(z | x) · pθ(y | x, z)

pη retriever: dense query and passage encoders (DPR) over Wikipedia

pθ generator: a seq2seq model (BART) that reads each passage

Σ marginalize over the top-k passages, trained end to end

Analogy first: closed-book exam = answer from memory; open-book = look it up, then answer. That’s the whole idea. Then the formal version from the paper that named it: Lewis et al. 2020 (“Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks”). Treat the retrieved passage z as a latent variable: the retriever gives a distribution over passages, the generator produces the answer given each passage, and you marginalize over the top-k. The retriever’s query encoder was fine-tuned jointly with the generator. Worth noting: most production “RAG” today doesn’t train anything end to end. It glues an off-the-shelf retriever to an off-the-shelf LLM through the prompt. Same idea, looser coupling.

Click through the builds. Top lane = indexing (offline): load docs, split them into chunks, embed each chunk, store vectors (and usually a keyword index too). Bottom lane = serving (online): optionally rewrite the question, embed it with the SAME embedding model, search for the top-k nearest chunks, rerank to a handful, stuff them into the prompt, generate with citations. Indexing · step 1

Chunking decides what can be found

Too big: one vector averages many ideas. Matches get blurry and you pay for tokens nobody needed.

Too small: facts lose their subject. A sentence about “the company” forgets which company.

Better: split on structure (headings, paragraphs), overlap a little, and carry the title and section with every chunk.

Fixed + overlap · recursive · semantic · late chunking

Chunking is the first decision and the hardest to undo: whatever information a chunk loses at index time, no downstream model can recover. Walk the animation: the document is split along the dashed lines; one chunk is pulled out; on its own it says “the company’s revenue grew 3%”. Which company? Which quarter? A query about ACME’s Q2 growth may never match it. Then the teal box appears: a short context sentence prepended to the chunk. That’s Anthropic’s “contextual retrieval” example; we’ll see its numbers in a few slides. Strategy names to drop: fixed-size with overlap (simple baseline), recursive splitting by headings/paragraphs/sentences, semantic splitting (cut where embedding similarity between sentences drops), late chunking (embed the whole doc with a long-context encoder, then pool per chunk).

Indexing · step 2

Meaning becomes geometry

An encoder maps any text to a vector of a few hundred to a few thousand numbers. Texts that mean similar things land close together, even with zero words in common.

cos θ = q · d / (‖q‖ ‖d‖)

Bi-encoder: documents are embedded once, offline. Only the query is embedded at request time. That’s what makes it fast.

Walk the animation: three topic clusters appear. A query (“I forgot how to sign in”) flies in and lands in the “account access” cluster. The dashed ring grabs its nearest neighbours. Then the vectors from the origin appear: similarity is the angle θ between the query vector and a document vector (“Resetting your password”). Note the two share no keywords. Technical points: typical dimensions range from 384 (small open models) to 3,072 (large API models). With normalized vectors, cosine similarity equals the dot product, which is why vector DBs often just use inner product. Must use the same model for documents and queries. Embedding models are trained contrastively: pull matching (query, passage) pairs together, push others apart. Caveat for later: “close in embedding space” means “topically similar”, not “true” or “answers the question”.

Search · step 3

Search without checking everyone

Brute force: every vector, every query

10⁹ vectors × 1,024 dims ≈ 10¹² ops

HNSW builds a layered “small-world” graph. Start in a sparse top layer, hop greedily toward the query, drop a layer, repeat. Roughly logarithmic hops.

The catch: it’s approximate. You trade a little recall for a lot of speed, and you tune that trade (M, efSearch).

Also: IVF (probe a few clusters) · PQ (compress vectors)

The numbers: exact nearest-neighbour search over a billion 1,024-dimensional vectors is on the order of a trillion multiply-adds for a single query. Not viable at interactive latency. Walk the animation: HNSW (Malkov & Yashunin, 2016) stacks graphs like a skip list. The top layer has few nodes with long-range links (“express lanes”). Greedy search moves to whichever neighbour is closer to the query, and when it can’t improve, it drops to the next, denser layer and continues. The bottom layer contains every vector. Parameters: M = links per node (memory vs. recall), efSearch = size of the candidate list during search (latency vs. recall). Other families: IVF clusters vectors and only searches a few clusters; product quantization compresses vectors so more fit in memory. Libraries: FAISS, and most vector databases use HNSW or IVF variants. Critical point: “approximate” means the true best chunk can be skipped. One more place retrieval silently fails.

Search · step 3b

Keywords and vectors fail in opposite places

QueryKeyword · BM25Dense vectors
“error E-4012 in v2.3”Wins: exact tokenMay miss it
“Priya Raman’s expense report”Wins: rare nameNames embed poorly
“how do I get my money back?”Misses “refund”Wins: same meaning
“¿cómo cancelo mi pedido?”Wrong languageWins: cross-lingual

So: do both, then fuse

RRF(d) = Σᵢ 1 / (k + rankᵢ(d))

k = 60

Reciprocal Rank Fusion uses ranks, not scores, so there’s no need to calibrate BM25 against cosine.

BM25 ≈ term frequency (with diminishing returns) × rarity of the term (IDF), normalized by document length.

Ask the room before revealing: “Which of these would a vector search get wrong?” Most people assume vectors beat keywords everywhere. Dense retrieval struggles with exact identifiers (error codes, SKUs, version numbers) and rare proper nouns, because the embedding smooths them into “something about errors”. BM25 can’t bridge vocabulary gaps (“money back” vs “refund”) or languages. Hybrid search runs both and fuses the lists. Reciprocal Rank Fusion (Cormack, Clarke & Büttcher, 2009): each list contributes 1/(k + rank); k = 60 damps the influence of top ranks. It’s robust because it ignores raw scores, which live on incomparable scales. Practical rule: hybrid + a reranker is the strong default baseline before trying anything fancy.

Search · step 4

Retrieve wide, then rerank narrow

Millions of chunks

~100 candidates

~5 in the prompt

Bi-encoder

enc(q) · enc(d)

Fast:documents encoded ahead of time.

Blind spot:query and document never see each other.

→ first-stage recall

Cross-encoder

model([q ; d]) → score

Accurate:full attention across the pair.

Slow:one forward pass per pair, so only for the top ~100.

→ reranking

Late interaction

Σᵢ maxⱼ sim(qᵢ, dⱼ)

Middle ground:a vector per token, matched at query time.

Cost:a much larger index.

→ ColBERT (2020)

The funnel is the mental model: cheap, high-recall retrieval narrows millions of chunks to ~100; an expensive, high-precision model narrows ~100 to the handful that go in the prompt. Bi-encoders compress each text into one vector independently, so they can’t model fine interactions (“refund NOT available after travel” vs “refund available”). A cross-encoder reads query and document together with full attention and outputs a relevance score. It’s much more accurate, but you can’t precompute anything, so it only runs on the shortlist. ColBERT (Khattab & Zaharia, 2020) keeps one vector per token. Score = for each query token, take its best-matching document token, sum over query tokens (“MaxSim”). Precomputable like a bi-encoder, finer-grained like a cross-encoder, but the index is many times larger. Tie back to Air Canada: the critical word in the policy was “before”. A cross-encoder is far more likely to notice that than a single averaged vector.

Case study · Anthropic, Sept 2024

Contextual retrieval

Before indexing, an LLM writes a short note situating each chunk in its document. The note is prepended for both the embedding and the BM25 index.

Prompt caching keeps it cheap: load the document once, reuse it for every chunk.

Read the fine print

Vendor-run benchmark. Metric = did the one “golden” chunk land in the top 20. Part of the gain is plain hybrid search.

Source: Anthropic, “Introducing Contextual Retrieval” (Sept 2024): failure rate 5.7% → 3.7% → 2.9% → 1.9%

This is the chunking problem from earlier, solved at index time. For every chunk, an LLM (they used a small Claude model) gets the full document plus the chunk and writes 50 to 100 tokens of context. That context is prepended, then the result is both embedded and added to the BM25 index. Numbers (top-20 failure rate, averaged across their datasets): 5.7% baseline → 3.7% with contextual embeddings (−35%) → 2.9% adding contextual BM25 (−49%) → 1.9% adding a reranker (−67%). Critique, which counts toward “your own analysis”: (1) it’s the vendor evaluating its own method; (2) the metric only checks whether one gold chunk appears in the top 20, not whether the final answer improved; (3) the jump from −35% to −49% mixes two things: adding context to BM25, and simply adding BM25 at all (hybrid search). A cleaner ablation would show hybrid-without-context. Still, the idea is simple, cheap with caching, and widely reproduced.

Generation · using the context

Lost in the middle

Models use evidence at the start and end of the context far better than evidence buried in the middle, long-context models included.

In the paper, GPT-3.5-Turbo with the answer mid-context scored below its no-documents baseline.

So, in practice

Send fewer, better chunks. Put the strongest evidence first. Rerank, then order on purpose. Ask for citations.

Liu et al., “Lost in the Middle: How Language Models Use Long Contexts”, TACL 2024. Curve shows the shape of the effect, not exact values.

Experiment: multi-document QA. Give the model 20 retrieved documents, exactly one of which contains the answer, and move that document from position 1 to 20. Accuracy traces a U: high when the answer is first (primacy), decent when last (recency), lowest in the middle. The striking result: for GPT-3.5-Turbo, putting the answer in the middle gave lower accuracy than giving it no documents at all (closed-book, about 56%). More context actively hurt. Newer models have improved middle-of-context recall, but follow-up work (e.g. Chroma’s 2025 “context rot” report across 18 models) still finds performance degrading as inputs get longer and noisier. Practical lesson: retrieval isn’t just about recall; precision and ordering matter because the model is not a perfect reader. Be explicit that the chart is a sketch of the shape, not the paper’s exact numbers.

Beyond naive RAG · fix the query

Users ask badly. Rewrite before you search.

HyDE

Have the LLM draft a hypothetical answer, then embed the draft instead of the question.

Why it works: answers look more like documents than questions do.

Gao et al., 2022

Rewrite

Turn a chat turn into a standalone query.

“what about students?”

→ “Does the Pro plan have a student discount?”

Decompose

Split a multi-hop question into searchable steps.

1 · who acquired GitHub?
2 · who is its CEO?
3 · when were they born?

Retrieval quality depends on the query, and real queries are short, vague, and conversational. HyDE (Gao et al., 2022, “Precise Zero-Shot Dense Retrieval without Relevance Labels”): generate a fake answer passage, embed it, search with that vector. The fake can contain errors. That’s fine: it’s only used to land in the right neighbourhood; the real documents provide the facts. Rewriting: in chat, “what about students?” is meaningless without history; resolve it into a standalone query before retrieving. Decomposition / multi-hop: “Was the CEO of the company that acquired GitHub born before 1970?” No single chunk answers it. Break it into sub-questions, retrieve for each, and chain. This is the bridge to agentic RAG in two slides. Cost: each rewrite is an extra LLM call before retrieval, which adds latency.

Beyond naive RAG · check the evidence

Don’t trust retrieval. Grade it.

Self-RAG · Asai et al., 2023

The model critiques itself

Fine-tuned to emit reflection tokens while it writes:

[Retrieve?] → [IsRelevant]
→ [IsSupported] → [IsUseful]

Retrieval becomes on-demand, and each claim is checked against its passage.

CRAG · Yan et al., 2024

A grader checks the evidence

A lightweight evaluator scores what came back:

Correct → refine, keep key strips
Incorrect → discard, search the web
Ambiguous → do both

Wraps around any existing pipeline.

Two influential ideas for making the system notice bad retrieval instead of confidently using it. Self-RAG (Asai et al., 2023): the LM is trained to generate special reflection tokens: whether to retrieve at all, whether a retrieved passage is relevant (ISREL), whether its own output is supported by the passage (ISSUP), and how useful the response is (ISUSE). At inference you can use these to filter or rank candidate continuations. Downside: requires training a model to speak this token vocabulary. Corrective RAG / CRAG (Yan et al., 2024): no change to the generator. A small retrieval evaluator assigns a confidence to the retrieved set; high → decompose and keep the relevant strips; low → throw it away and fall back to web search; in between → combine. The shared idea: retrieval quality is checked at runtime, not assumed. In production this often shows up as a simple LLM-as-judge “is this context sufficient to answer?” step.

Beyond naive RAG · structure

GraphRAG: global questions

Local: “What’s our refund window?” One chunk has it.
Global: “What do customers complain about most, across 10,000 tickets?” No chunk has it.

  1. An LLM extracts entities and relationships from every chunk.
  2. Build a graph and detect communities (Leiden).
  3. Pre-write a summary of each community.
  4. Answer global questions by map-reducing over the summaries.

Indexing is LLM-heavy and expensive: great for sensemaking, overkill for lookups.

Edge et al., “From Local to Global: A Graph RAG Approach to Query-Focused Summarization”, Microsoft Research, 2024

Classic RAG is built for needle-in-a-haystack questions. Ask it a “sensemaking” question about the whole corpus and top-k retrieval just returns k random-ish complaints. GraphRAG (Microsoft Research, Edge et al., 2024): at index time an LLM reads every chunk and extracts entities (people, products, issues) and relationships. That forms a knowledge graph. Community detection (Leiden algorithm) finds clusters of tightly connected entities (the dashed circles). The LLM writes a summary of each community, at several levels of granularity. At query time, global questions are answered by asking each community summary for a partial answer and then combining (map-reduce). Critique: indexing costs many LLM calls per chunk, and extraction errors become baked into the graph. Great for exploratory analysis of a fixed corpus; usually overkill for FAQ-style lookups.

Beyond naive RAG · where it’s heading

Agentic RAG

The retriever becomes a tool. The model decides when to search, what to search for, and when it has enough.

GAINS

Multi-hop questions, choosing among sources, recovering from a bad first search.

COSTS

Several LLM calls per question: more latency and tokens. Errors compound, so evaluate the whole trajectory, not just the final answer.

This is the direction most production systems moved in through 2025 and 2026: instead of “retrieve once, then generate”, the LLM runs a loop with retrieval as one of its tools. It plans, searches, reads what came back, judges whether it has enough evidence, and either searches again with a refined query or answers. Connects everything: query rewriting and decomposition become the “Plan” step; self-grading (Self-RAG / CRAG ideas) becomes the “Enough?” step; and the search tool can be a vector index, a SQL database, or the web. Trade-off: noticeably better on multi-hop and research-style questions, but slower and more expensive per question, and harder to evaluate: you need to look at which searches it ran, not just the answer. For simple FAQ lookups, single-shot RAG is still faster and cheaper.

The recurring debate

Did long context kill RAG?

Stuff the whole windowRetrieve first
Cost per queryPay for every token, every timeBetter:a few thousand tokens
LatencyGrows with context lengthBetter:small prompt
Data that changes dailyRebuild the promptBetter:update the index
Per-user permissionsHard to enforceBetter:filter at search time
Reasoning across one whole documentBetter:sees everythingCan miss connections
Small, stable corpusBetter:simplest optionUnneeded machinery

Today’s default is both: retrieve generously, then let a long-context model reason over what came back.

Every time context windows grow, someone declares RAG dead. Ask the room for a show of hands: “Who thinks RAG will still matter in 3 years?” The honest answer is “it depends on the workload”. Long context wins when the material is one document or a small, stable set and the task needs reasoning across all of it. Retrieval wins on cost (you’re not paying to re-read the whole corpus on every query), latency, freshness, access control (user A must never see user B’s documents), and citations. And “lost in the middle” / context-rot results mean a bigger window isn’t the same as better use of it. The convergence: retrieval narrows a huge corpus to a generous candidate set (whole sections rather than tiny chunks), and a long-context model reasons over it. Prompt caching blurs the line further for frequently reused documents.

Evaluation

Debug it like a ladder

1

Is the answer in the corpus at all?

coverage

2

Did retrieval find it?

recall@k · MRR · nDCG

3

Did it survive into the prompt?

rerank cutoff · truncation

4

Is the answer faithful to that context?

faithfulness · groundedness

5

Does it actually answer the question?

answer relevance

Start with 50 to 100real questions and their correct sources. LLM judges (e.g. RAGAS) scale this up, but spot-check the judge by hand.

The most useful practical slide. “The RAG system gave a wrong answer” is not a diagnosis. Walk down the ladder: if the answer isn’t in the corpus, no retriever can help (that’s a content problem). If it’s in the corpus but not in the top-k, it’s a retrieval problem: chunking, embeddings, hybrid, reranking. If it was retrieved but cut before the prompt, it’s a reranker threshold or context budget problem. If it was in the prompt but the model ignored or contradicted it, it’s a generation problem (lost in the middle, prompt, model). If it’s faithful but doesn’t answer the question, the query understanding is the issue. Metric definitions: recall@k = fraction of questions whose gold source is in the top k. MRR = mean of 1/rank of the first relevant hit. nDCG = ranking quality with graded relevance, discounting lower positions. Faithfulness = are the answer’s claims supported by the retrieved context. Answer relevance = does it address the question. Apply it to Air Canada: rung 1 passed (the policy page existed). The failure was at rung 2, 3 or 4, and that’s exactly what our demo reproduced.

My take

Three things RAG doesn’t fix

01

It moves hallucination; it doesn’t remove it

Retrieve a relevant but outdated page and you get a confident, cited, wrong answer. Citations raise trust faster than they raise accuracy.

02

The corpus is the new attack surface

Any indexed document can carry hidden instructions or poisoned facts. Retrieval must enforce the same permissions as the documents.

03

Benchmarks grade the easy part

“Was the golden chunk in the top 20?” ignores real corpora that contradict themselves, like an old policy page next to a new one.

Grounding helps when the right document is retrieved. When a plausible-but-wrong document is retrieved, the model now has “evidence” and a citation, which makes the wrong answer more convincing.
